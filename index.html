The Gap Between
Knowing and Deciding

Companies today use machine learning to forecast things like electricity prices, hospital demand, and delivery volumes. They feed those forecasts into planning software that makes decisions. The problem is that the forecasting model is trained to get the numbers right, not to help the planner make better decisions. Those are two very different things. This research builds and tests methods that fix that gap, across three real industries.

The core idea. When a machine learning model makes a prediction mistake, standard training treats all mistakes the same. But in the real world, some mistakes are very costly and others do not matter at all. A wrong electricity price forecast at 3am changes nothing for a battery. The same mistake at 6pm can cause the battery to charge when it should be selling, losing hundreds of euros. We build a better training method called SPO+ that makes the model focus its effort on the mistakes that actually lead to bad decisions. We test this idea across three industries that Maastricht University works with directly.

The Research Problem

Why Getting the Forecast Right
Does Not Mean Making Good Decisions

Most planning systems split the work into two steps: first predict, then decide. That split is where the problem hides.

The flaw in standard machine learning

How it works today

A machine learning model makes a forecast. A planning tool takes that forecast and works out the best decision. The model is trained to be accurate. The planning tool is designed to optimise. But they never talk to each other during training.

This only works well if every forecast mistake is equally bad. In real operations, they are not. For a hospital, being short of nurses by two people costs four times more than having two extra nurses sitting idle. Standard training does not know that. It treats both mistakes exactly the same.

The result: a model that looks accurate on paper but makes bad planning decisions in practice.

The better approach

What SPO+ does differently

Two researchers at Cornell (Elmachtoub and Grigas, 2022) proposed a different way to train forecasting models. Instead of asking "how close was my prediction to the truth?", ask "did my prediction lead to a good decision?"

This is straightforward when the planning tool is a linear program, because you can calculate exactly which prediction mistakes changed the final decision. It becomes a research challenge when the planning tool is a routing algorithm for delivery trucks, because those are too complex to work backwards through.

That research challenge is what makes this PhD interesting. The three case studies below each expose a different version of the same core problem.

How the Research Works

The Same Five Steps, Three Times Over

All three case studies follow the same process. The only thing that changes is Step 3, and that is where the interesting research lives.

Step 1

Collect Data

Real or calibrated data from each industry. Electricity prices, hospital admissions, parcel volumes by postcode.

→
Step 2

Train a Forecast Model

XGBoost trained first the standard way (minimise error), then retrained the SPO+ way (minimise bad decisions).

→
Step 3 · The research question

Run the Planner

Energy and hospital use a linear program. Logistics uses a routing algorithm. Only one of these can be taught to give feedback to the forecast model.

→
Step 4

Make the Decision

Charge or sell the battery. Call in nurses. Plan the delivery routes. Each decision is made using the forecast.

→
Step 5

Measure the Cost

Compare the outcome to what would have happened with a perfect forecast. That gap is what we are trying to close.

⚡

What changes when SPO+ is in charge of training

In Step 2, instead of the model learning "I was 8 euros off on this prediction", it learns "my mistake at 6pm caused the battery to charge instead of sell, which cost 200 euros". The model pays more attention to hours where errors flip decisions and less to hours where the outcome would have been the same either way. How much that helps in practice depends on whether those high-impact hours have a pattern the model can learn. When they do, SPO+ clearly wins. When forecast errors are spread uniformly across all hours, the gain shrinks. The PhD will measure exactly where that boundary sits, across energy, hospital, and logistics data, and across different market conditions.

Case Study 1 · Energy

A Battery Trading Electricity on the Dutch Market

A 1 MW battery buys electricity when prices are low and sells when they are high. To do this it needs to know tomorrow's prices today. We forecast those prices with machine learning, feed them into a linear program that decides when to charge and sell, then measure how much money was lost compared to a perfect forecast.

What we predict
Hourly electricity price
Planner type
Linear program
Data source
ENTSO-E, 34,320 hours
What we measure
Revenue lost vs perfect
SPO+ and standard XGBoost earn similar revenue here because 93% of hours are decision-sensitive, leaving no specific cluster to target. That is the finding: SPO+ gains depend on market structure, not just the method. Measuring when that changes is the research question.

Case Study 2 · Hospital

Staffing an ICU Ward When Patient Numbers Are Uncertain

A teaching hospital forecasts how many patients will arrive in the ICU tomorrow and uses that number to plan how many nurses to call in. Being short-staffed costs four times more than having too many nurses. The standard model does not know about that imbalance when it is learning to predict.

What we predict
Patient admissions
Planner type
Linear program with penalty
Data source
LCPS, NZa, CBS
What we measure
Extra staffing cost
SPO+ here learns to be cautious. Rather than being equally accurate up and down, it learns that guessing slightly high is much safer than guessing slightly low, because the consequences are so different.

Case Study 3 · Logistics

Planning Delivery Routes When Parcel Demand Is Unknown

A Dutch delivery company forecasts how many parcels will need to go to each neighbourhood tomorrow and uses that to plan its van routes. Unlike the battery and the hospital, there is no clean mathematical formula for the best route at this scale. The planner uses shortcuts instead.

What we predict
Parcels per postcode zone
Planner type
Routing algorithm (VRP)
Data source
CBS, OpenStreetMap NL
What we measure
Extra kilometres driven
This is the hardest case. The routing algorithm cannot easily give feedback to the forecast model. Figuring out how to make SPO+ work here anyway is the most novel part of the research.

What This Research Will Build

Four Things That Do Not Exist Yet

The three demos show the problem is real and worth solving. The PhD will produce four concrete things that are new to the field.

  • 1
    SPO+ that understands time The original SPO+ paper treats each prediction independently. But battery charging carries over from hour to hour, and hospital shifts overlap. We extend the method to handle this kind of chain reaction across time.
  • 2
    SPO+ for planning tools that use shortcuts When the planner is a routing algorithm instead of a linear program, SPO+ cannot work directly. We develop and test ways to approximate the feedback signal so the training still works.
  • 3
    Explaining decisions, not just predictions Most explainability tools tell you why the model made a certain prediction. We build tools that tell you why the model made a certain decision, which is what managers actually care about.
  • 4
    A public benchmark across three industries A reusable dataset and comparison of methods that other researchers can build on, covering energy, healthcare, and logistics with real Dutch data.

Transfer Case · Hospital Operations

Getting the Nurse Count Wrong
Costs More Than You Think

A hospital forecasts how many patients will need intensive care tomorrow and uses that number to decide how many nurses to call in. Getting the forecast wrong in either direction costs money. But the costs are very different. Having too many nurses on a quiet day wastes money. Not having enough nurses on a busy day means patients wait longer for care and the hospital pays emergency rates to fix it.

The two costs are not the same Standard machine learning treats getting the number too high and getting it too low as equally bad mistakes. But a hospital does not work that way. Calling in one extra nurse costs about 45 euros an hour. Being one nurse short costs closer to 180 euros an hour in emergency cover. A model trained to reduce the number of mistakes does not know about this difference. A model trained to reduce the total cost would.

Staffing decisions have to be made a day early ICU shifts are usually locked in 12 to 24 hours before they start. Monday morning admissions can vary by about 40% depending on how many patients left over the weekend. A model trained to reduce cost rather than just prediction errors should get better at recognising these patterns.

The Growing Cost Gap

A few bad days drive most of the cost Having too many nurses adds a small steady cost each day. Being short-staffed is rare but hits the budget hard when it happens. In this model a small number of days where a patient surge was missed explains most of the total overspend.

Why standard training gets this wrong Standard training tells the model to be accurate. It treats a prediction that was 2 patients too high the same as a prediction that was 2 patients too low. But in a hospital they cost very different amounts. Being understaffed costs about 180 euros per patient-hour. Being overstaffed costs about 45 euros per hour. SPO+ (Elmachtoub & Grigas, 2022) trains the model on the actual cost of each type of mistake, so it learns to worry more about underestimates during busy morning and afternoon windows.

Feature Engineering & Importances

The model was trained on real Dutch ICU admission data from RIVM, covering October 2021 to April 2024. The national figures were scaled down to match a single teaching hospital with 40 beds, which represents about 3.5% of national ICU capacity. The model predicts how many patients will be in the ICU tomorrow. That number feeds into the staffing planner. Importance shows how much each input variable helps the model on average.
